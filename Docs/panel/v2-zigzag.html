Yes — 6 HP fits, and stretching the zigzag makes it generous. Staggering the toggles left and right of a centred LED column breaks the rule that killed the single-column layout: vertical pitch no longer has to clear a toggle nut, because vertically adjacent toggles are on opposite sides of the panel. With the bus block moved to the very bottom, the channel zigzag stretches to fill everything above it — same-side toggles now sit 17.25 mm apart, which is more finger room than any of the earlier two-column layouts had, in half the width.

Lifting each LED 2.5 mm above its toggle's row helps, but less than you'd hope: the gain is diagonal, not linear, so the web of panel between nut and LED hole goes from 1.1 mm to 1.6 mm. Better — still thin. At 6 HP the horizontal squeeze is real, and the toggle you buy decides it, not the layout.

What's drawn, and what to watch

The layout. LEDs run straight down the centreline, one per channel, each riding 2.5 mm above its toggle's row with the channel number silkscreened directly beneath it. That numbered anchor is what makes the alternation readable — the LED sits 2.5 mm from its own switch and 6.1 mm from the one above, so it reads as belonging to the nearer one, and the number settles any doubt. Toggles alternate left, right, left, right — throwing horizontally, so left routes that pulse to Bus A and right to Bus B, centre off. The EXT jack continues the zigzag as the ninth position, one full step below channel 8. Below the rule, the two buses sit side by side: output jacks at the very bottom, their mode toggles directly above throwing vertically (up = OR, down = AND), and the bus LED above that. Vertical throw on the bus switches also means they never get confused with the routing switches by feel.

Pitch is set by the EXT jack. Landing it one full zigzag step below channel 8 and clearing the bus LEDs puts it at y = 82, and that back-solves the channel pitch to 8.625 mm — so the zigzag fills the panel exactly rather than being padded to fit.

Two body-clearance checks, both behind the panel. The first is the one that moved: a vertical-throw toggle sitting directly above a jack puts a ~13 mm-tall switch body above a ~12.5 mm-tall jack body. At the 14 mm pitch drawn here they miss each other by about 1.25 mm — real, but thin. The second is unchanged: the channel toggles, rotated 90° for horizontal throw, present that same ~13 mm dimension across the panel, and at 6 HP the columns are only 14.5 mm apart on centre. Measure your actual switch before committing; the front looks fine long before the back does.

Switch selected: Adam Tech SW-T2-4X-E-A2-MA2 — DPDT ON-OFF-ON, M6 × 0.75 bushing, PCB vertical. DPDT is an electrical requirement, not a preference: a single-pole switch leaks the AND bus into the OR bus through its unselected throw. That's a miniature bushing rather than sub-miniature, so the ø6.00 mm hole above is exactly right and the 1.6 mm nut-to-LED gap stands. Behind the panel, the 13.2 mm body against the 14.5 mm column pitch leaves 1.3 mm between adjacent bodies — tight, but it clears.

Panel gets the bushing hole only — no anti-rotation key hole. The datasheet's recommended cut-out adds a ø2.40 mm key hole 6.40 mm from the bushing centre, which would overlap the channel LED hole outright. The six solder pins stop the switch rotating once it's PCB-mounted, so it isn't needed. Don't copy the datasheet cut-out into the panel DXF.

Still to measure: the nut. The datasheet gives the bushing thread but not the hex nut's across-corners dimension — and that is the number that sets the 1.6 mm gap. Standard M6 toggle nuts run ~9–11 mm across corners; at 11 mm the gap collapses to ~0.7 mm and 6 HP is off. That one measurement picks 6 HP or 8 HP.
